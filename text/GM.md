# The GM

There are many different genres, each with their own style or advice for GMing. Death World: Catachan is designed for one of those styles in particular—a grim world of survival, horror, and constant danger where every step could be your last, where every breath could be your final one, where the darkness hungers for the souls of the faithful. The rules in this chapter will help you run a game in that style, to portray a universe where hope is a luxury and death is a constant companion.

The characters have rules to follow when they roll dice and take actions, their fate hanging on the whims of chance and the will of the Emperor. The GM has rules to follow, too, for you are the voice of the darkness, the herald of horror, the one who brings the endless terror of the Death World to life. You will be refereeing, adjudicating, and describing the world as you go—Death World: Catachan provides a framework to guide you in doing so, to help you portray a world where every moment is a struggle against the darkness.

This chapter is not about advice for the GM or optional tips and tricks on how best to play Death World: Catachan. It is a chapter with procedures and rules for whoever takes on the role of GM, the sacred duty of manifesting the unending terror of the Death World, of being the voice of the jungle that craves human flesh.

### GMing Death World: Catachan—A Framework

Running a game of Death World: Catachan is built on these: the GM's**agenda**,**principles**, and**moves**. The agenda is what you set out to do when you sit down at the table, the dark purpose that drives you to manifest the unending terror of the Death World. The principles are the guides that keep you focused on that agenda, the sacred tenets that ensure the shadows remain constant and the horror never relents. The GM's moves are the concrete, moment-to-moment things you do to move the game forward, the tools you use to bring death and despair to the characters' lives. You will make moves when players miss their rolls, when the rules call for it, and whenever the players look to you to see what happens, to see what new horror awaits them. Your moves keep the fiction consistent and the game's action moving forward, ensuring that the perpetual conflict for survival never ceases.

The GM's agenda, principles, and moves are rules just like damage or stats or Wounds. You should take the same care in altering them or ignoring them that you would with any other rule, for they are the foundation upon which the endless horror of the Death World is built.

## How to GM

When you sit down at the table as a GM you do these things:

- Describe the situation
- Follow the rules
- Make moves
- Exploit your prep

The players have it easy—they just say what their characters say, think, and do, their lives hanging on the edge of a knife. You have it harder, for you must say everything else, you must be the voice of the darkness, the herald of horror, the one who brings the endless terror of the Death World to life. What does that entail?

First and foremost, you**describe the immediate situation around the players at all times**. This is how you start a session, how you get things rolling after a snack break, get back on track after a digression: tell them what the situation is in concrete terms, paint a picture of the horror that awaits them.

Use detail and senses to draw them in, to make them feel the oppressive weight of the jungle, the stench of death on the wind, the sound of something hungry moving in the darkness. The situation is not just an Ork charging you, it is an Ork painted in blood swinging a choppa and yelling "Waaagh!" its eyes burning with the fury of the greenskin. You can leverage a lack of information, too. The sound of something large moving through the undergrowth, the smell of something dead nearby, the oppressive silence that means something is watching, waiting, hunting.

The situation around them is rarely "everything's great, nothing to worry about." They are soldiers on a Death World—give them something to react to, something to fear, something that hungers for their death. The jungle is always hungry, always watching, always waiting for the moment to strike.

When you describe the situation, always end with "What do you do?" Death World: Catachan is about survival and action, about the ceaseless battle against the shadows! Portray a situation that demands a response, that forces them to act, that gives them no choice but to face the horror that awaits them.

From the get-go make sure to**follow the rules**. This means your GM rules, sure, but also keep an eye on the players’ moves. It’s everyone’s responsibility to watch for when a move has been triggered, including you. Stop the players and ask if they mean to trigger the rules when it sounds like that’s what they’re doing.

Part of following the rules is**making moves**. Your moves are different than player moves and we’ll describe them in detail in a bit. Your moves are specific things you can do to change the flow of the game.

In all of these things,**exploit your prep**. At times you'll know something the players don't yet know. You can use that knowledge to help you make moves. Maybe the scout tries to use their vox-caster and draws unwanted attention. They don't know that the attention that just fell on them was the ominous gaze of a Catachan Devil waiting in the trees ahead, but you do.

## Agenda

Your agenda makes up the things you aim to do at all times while GMing a game of Death World: Catachan:

- Portray a grim world
- Fill the characters' lives with danger
- Play to find out what happens

Everything you say and do at the table (and away from the table, too) exists to accomplish these three goals and no others. Things that are not on this list are not your goals. You are not trying to beat the players or test their ability to solve complex traps. You are not here to give the players a chance to explore your finely crafted setting. You are not trying to kill the players (though the jungle and its creatures might be, and the Warp itself certainly hungers for their souls). You are most certainly not here to tell everyone a planned-out story, for the story writes itself in blood and suffering.

Your first agenda is to**portray a grim world**. Death World: Catachan is all about guts, guile, and survival against darkness and doom, about the perpetual conflict between humanity and the horrors that crave their souls. It is about characters who have decided to take up a life of service in the Imperial Guard, fighting on a Death World where every step could be their last, where every breath could be their final one. It is your job to participate in that by showing the players a world in which their characters must struggle to survive, where hope is a luxury and death is a constant companion. Without the player characters the outposts would fall to the jungle or its predators—it might still even with them, for the darkness is patient and the jungle is endless. It is up to you to portray the grim elements of that world. Show the players the horrors of the world they are in and encourage them to react to it, to face the darkness with faith and fury.

**Filling the characters' lives with danger**means working with the players to create a world that is engaging and dynamic, where every moment is a struggle against the darkness. Soldiers are always caught up in some mission-threatening danger or another—encourage and foster that kind of action in the game, for it is the only way they will survive. The jungle is always hungry. The threats are always real. The darkness never relents.

Death World: Catachan missions**never**presume player actions. A Death World: Catachan mission portrays a setting in motion—someplace significant with creatures big and small pursuing their own goals, their own hungers, their own dark purposes. As the players come into conflict with that setting and its denizens, action is inevitable, and that action will have consequences. You will honestly portray the repercussions of that action, for the world does not care about the characters' plans or desires.

This is how you**play to find out what happens**. You are sharing in the tension of finding out how the characters react to and survive in the world you are portraying, how they face the endless horror and whether they will emerge victorious or be consumed by the darkness. You are all participants in a grim struggle that is unfolding, a story written in blood and suffering. So really, do not plan too hard. The rules of the game will fight you. It is fun to see how things unfold, to watch the characters struggle against the darkness and discover whether they will survive or fall. Trust in the darkness, and let it guide you.

## Principles

- Draw maps, leave blanks
- Address the characters, not the players
- Embrace the fantastic
- Make a move that follows
- Never speak the name of your move
- Give every monster life
- Name every person
- Ask questions and use the answers
- Be a fan of the characters
- Think dangerous
- Begin and end with the fiction
- Think offscreen, too

Your principles are your guides. Often, when it’s time to make a move, you’ll already have an idea of what makes sense. Consider it in light of your principles and go with it, if it fits.

#### Draw maps, leave blanks

Death World: Catachan exists mostly in the imaginations of the people playing it; maps help everyone stay on the same page. You won’t always be drawing them yourself, but any time there’s a new location described make sure it gets added to a map.

When you draw a map don’t try to make it complete. Leave room for the unknown. As you play you’ll get more ideas and the players will give you inspiration to work with. Let the maps expand and change.

#### Address the characters, not the players

Addressing the characters, not the players, means that you don’t say, “Tony, is Dunwick doing something about that wight?” Instead you say, “Dunwick, what are you doing about the wight?” Speaking this way keeps the game focused on the fiction and not on the table. It’s important to the flow of the game, too. If you talk to the players you may leave out details that are important to what moves the characters make. Since moves are always based on the actions of the character you need to think about what’s happening in terms of those characters—not the players portraying them.

#### Embrace the grim

The Warp, that realm of madness and corruption, strange vistas that should not exist, the God-Emperor of Mankind, daemons that hunger for mortal souls, and abominations that defy comprehension: the world is full of horror and danger, and you must embrace that in your prep and in play. Think about "the grim" on various scales. Think about massive hive cities where billions toil in endless misery, or worlds consumed by the Tyranids, their populations devoured by the endless hunger of the Great Devourer. Think about jungle shamans and their knowledge of deadly plants, or the ancient ruins that the local cultists use for dark rituals, places where the Warp bleeds through into reality. The characters are interesting people, empowered by their faith in the Emperor, their skill at arms, or by their training in the ways of death. The world should be just as engaging—and just as deadly, a place where every moment is a struggle against the darkness, where hope is a luxury and death is a constant companion.

#### Make a move that follows

When you make a move what you’re actually doing is taking an element of the fiction and bringing it to bear against the characters. Your move should always follow from the fiction. They help you focus on one aspect of the current situation and do something interesting with it. What’s going on? What move makes sense here?

#### Never speak the name of your move

There is no quicker way to ruin the consistency of Death World: Catachan than to tell the players what move you’re making. Your moves are prompts to you, not things you say directly.

You never show the players that you're picking a move from a list. You know the reason the Catachan Devil dragged off Jackson was because you made the "put someone in a spot" move, but you show it to the players as a straightforward outcome of their actions, since it is.

#### Give every enemy life

Enemies are dangerous creatures with their own motivations (simple or complex). Give each enemy details that bring it to life: smells, sights, sounds. Give each one enough to make it real, but don't cry when it gets beat up or overthrown. That's what player characters do!

#### Name every person

Anyone that the players speak with has a name. They probably have a personality and some goals or opinions too, but you can figure that out as you go. Start with a name. The rest can flow from there.

#### Ask questions and use the answers

Part of playing to find out what happens is explicitly not knowing everything, and being curious. If you don’t know something, or you don’t have an idea, ask the players and use what they say.

The easiest question to use is "What do you do?" Whenever you make a move, end with "What do you do?" You don't even have to ask the person you made the move against. Take that chance to shift the focus elsewhere: "Marbo's lasgun jams with a click. Rock, you were counting on that covering fire. What are you doing now that it's gone?"

#### Be a fan of the characters

Think of the players’ characters as protagonists in a story you might see on TV. Cheer for their victories and lament their defeats. You’re not here to push them in any particular direction, merely to participate in fiction that features them and their action.

#### Think dangerous

Everything in the world is a target, a potential victim of the endless hunger that defines existence on a Death World. You are thinking like the jungle itself: no single life is worth anything and there is nothing sacrosanct, nothing that cannot be destroyed, nothing that cannot be consumed. Everything can be put in danger, everything can be destroyed, everything can be reduced to nothing more than food for the endless hunger. Nothing you create is ever protected, for the darkness cares nothing for your plans or desires. Whenever your eye falls on something you have created, think how it can be put in danger, fall apart or crumble, how it can be consumed by the endless horror. The world changes, and it always changes for the worse. Without the characters' intervention, it changes for the worse, descending deeper into darkness and despair. The jungle is always hungry, always watching, always waiting for the moment to strike.

#### Begin and end with the fiction

Everything you and the players do in Death World: Catachan comes from and leads to fictional events. When the players make a move, they take a fictional action to trigger it, apply the rules, and get a fictional effect. When you make a move it always comes from the fiction.

#### Think offscreen too

Just because you’re a fan of the characters doesn’t mean everything happens right in front of them. Sometimes your best move is in the next room, or another part of the dungeon, or even back in town. Make your move elsewhere and show its effects when they come into the spotlight.

## Moves

Whenever everyone looks to you to see what happens choose one of these. Each move is something that occurs in the fiction of the game—they aren’t code words or special terms. “Use up their resources” literally means to expend the resources of the characters, for example.

- Use an enemy, danger, or location move
- Reveal an unwelcome truth
- Show signs of an approaching threat
- Deal damage
- Use up their resources
- Turn their move back on them
- Separate them
- Give an opportunity that fits a class' abilities
- Show a downside to their class, race, or equipment
- Offer an opportunity, with or without cost
- Put someone in a spot
- Tell them the requirements or consequences and ask
- Test their resolve
- Show the cost of survival
- Corrupt or tempt

Never speak the name of your move (that is one of your principles). Make it a real thing that happens to them, a concrete event in the endless horror: "As you dodge the hulking Catachan Devil's claw, you slip and land hard, the impact driving the air from your lungs. Your chainsword goes sliding away into the undergrowth, lost in the darkness. You think you saw where it went but the Devil is turning your way, its eyes burning with hunger, its claws dripping with the blood of your fallen comrades. What do you do?"

No matter what move you make, always follow up with "What do you do?" Your moves are a way of fulfilling your agenda—part of which is to fill the characters' lives with danger, to ensure that every moment is a struggle against the darkness. When a lasgun jams or the ground gives way beneath them soldiers react or suffer the consequences of inaction, for hesitation on Catachan is death, and death is the only certainty in the endless darkness.

### When to Make a Move

You make a move:

- When everyone looks to you to find out what happens
- When the players give you a golden opportunity
- When they roll a 6-

Generally when the players are just looking at you to find out what happens you make a soft move, otherwise you make a hard move.

A soft move is one without immediate, irrevocable consequences. That usually means it’s something not all that bad, like revealing that there’s more treasure if they can just find a way past the golem (offer an opportunity with cost). It can also mean that it’s something bad, but they have time to avoid it, like having the goblin archers loose their arrows (show signs of an approaching threat) with a chance for them to dodge out of danger.

A soft move ignored becomes a golden opportunity for a hard move. If the players do nothing about the hail of arrows flying towards them it’s a golden opportunity to use the deal damage move.

Hard moves, on the other hand, have immediate consequences. Dealing damage is almost always a hard move, since it means a loss of Wounds that won’t be recovered without some action from the players.

When you have a chance to make a hard move you can opt for a soft one instead if it better fits the situation. Sometimes things just work out for the best, sometimes the Emperor's grace shines through the darkness—but such moments are rare, and they should be treasured, for they are the exception, not the rule.

### Choosing a Move

To choose a move, start by looking at the obvious consequences of the action that triggered it. If you already have an idea, think on it for a second to make sure it fits your agenda and principles and then do it.**Let your moves snowball**. Build on the success or failure of the characters’ moves and on your own previous moves.

If your first instinct is that this won’t hurt them now, but it’ll come back to bite them later, great! That’s part of your principles (think offscreen too). Make a note of and reveal it when the time is right.

### Making your Move

When making a move, keep your principles in mind. In particular, never speak the name of your move and address the characters, not the players. Your moves are not mechanical actions happening around the table. They are concrete events happening to the characters in the fictional world you are describing.

Note that “deal damage” is a move, but other moves may include damage as well. When an ogre flings you against a wall you take damage as surely as if he had smashed you with his fists.

After every move you make, always ask “What do you do?”

#### Use an enemy, danger, or location move

Every enemy in a mission has moves associated with it, as do many locations. An enemy or location move is just a description of what that location or enemy does, maybe "hurl someone away" or "strike from ambush." If a player move (like jungle fury) says that an enemy gets to make an attack, make an aggressive move with that enemy.

The overarching dangers of the mission also have moves associated with them. Use these moves to bring that danger into play, which may mean more enemies.

#### Reveal an unwelcome truth

An unwelcome truth is a fact the players wish was not true, a revelation that shatters their hopes and plunges them deeper into darkness: that the outpost has been compromised, maybe, or that the helpful scout is actually a genestealer cultist, their body a vessel for the xenos filth that hungers for human flesh. Reveal to the players just how much trouble they are in, how deep the darkness runs, how hopeless their situation truly is.

#### Show signs of an approaching threat

This is one of your most versatile moves. "Threat" means anything bad that is on the way, anything that hungers for the characters' death. With this move, you just show them that something is going to happen unless they do something about it, that the darkness is closing in, that death is approaching, that the endless horror is about to claim another victim.

#### Deal damage

When you deal damage, choose one source of damage that is fictionally threatening a character and apply it, let the darkness claim its due. In combat with a Catachan Devil? It tears into you, its claws ripping through armor and flesh alike. Triggered a trap? The ground collapses beneath you, plunging you into darkness and death.

The amount of damage is decided by the source, by the horror that inflicts it. In some cases, this move might involve trading damage both ways, with the character also dealing damage, a desperate exchange of blows in the ceaseless battle for survival.

Most damage is based on a die roll. When a player takes damage, tell them what to roll. You never need to touch the dice. If the player is too cowardly to find out their own fate, they can ask another player to roll for them, to face the darkness in their stead.

#### Use up their resources

Surviving on Catachan, or anywhere dangerous, often comes down to supplies, to the meager resources that stand between life and death. With this move, something happens to use up some resource: weapons, armor, healing, ammunition. You do not always have to use it up permanently. A lasgun might just be flung into the undergrowth, not destroyed, but lost in the darkness, its absence a constant reminder of the price of survival.

#### Turn Their Move Back On Them

Think about the benefits a move might grant a character and turn them around in a negative way. Alternately, grant the same advantage to someone who has it out for the characters. If Jackson has learned of the Ork warband approaching from the east, maybe a scout has spotted him, too.

#### Separate Them

There are few things worse than being in the middle of a raging battle with blood-thirsty Catachan Devils on all sides—one of those things is being in the middle of that battle with no one at your back.

Separating the characters can mean anything from being pushed apart in the heat of battle to being lost in the jungle. Whatever way it occurs, it's bound to cause problems. On Catachan, being alone is often a death sentence.

#### Give an opportunity that fits a specialization's abilities

The sniper disables traps, sneaks, and picks locks. The field medic deals with wounds and toxins. Every specialization has things that they shine at—present an opportunity that plays to what one specialization shines at.

It doesn't have to be a specialization that's in play right now though. Sometimes a locked door stands between you and salvage and there's no sniper in sight. This is an invitation for invention, bargaining, and creativity. If all you've got is a chainsword doesn't every problem look like something to cut?

#### Show a downside to their specialization, equipment, or situation

Just as every specialization shines, they all have their weaknesses too. Do Orks have a special thirst for Imperial blood? Is the field medic's treatment attracting dangerous predators? The lasgun that lights the way also draws attention from eyes in the dark.

#### Offer an opportunity, with or without cost

Show them something they want: salvage, respect, survival. If you want, you can associate some cost with it too, of course.

Remember to lead with the fiction. You don't say, "This area isn't dangerous so you can establish perimeter here, if you're willing to take the time." You make it a solid fictional thing and say, "The Emperor's blessings still hang around the shattered outpost. It's a relatively safe spot, but the sounds from the jungle are getting closer. What do you do?"

#### Put someone in a spot

A spot is someplace where a character needs to make tough choices. Put them, or something they care about, in the path of destruction. The harder the choice, the tougher the spot.

#### Tell them the requirements or consequences and ask

This move is particularly good when they want something that's not covered by a move, or they've failed a move. They can do it, sure, but they'll have to pay the price. Or, they can do it, but there will be consequences. Maybe they can swim through the venom-snake infested swamp before being devoured, but they'll need a distraction. Of course, this is made clear to the characters, not just the players: the snakes are in a feeding frenzy, for example.

#### Test their resolve

The horrors of Catachan are not merely physical—they are psychological, spiritual, and soul-crushing. Use this move to apply psychological pressure: show them the true cost of their actions, force them to witness atrocities, expose them to Warp corruption, or make them face impossible choices. This is when you trigger Hold Your Nerve, when you show them something that should break them, when you make them question their faith, their purpose, or their sanity. The darkness does not just kill—it corrupts, it tortures, it breaks the spirit long before it claims the body.

#### Show the cost of survival

Every victory on Catachan comes at a price. Use this move to highlight the toll that survival takes: show them the scars they've accumulated, the friends they've lost, the innocence they've sacrificed, the humanity they've left behind. Remind them of what they've done to survive, of the compromises they've made, of the darkness that has seeped into their souls. Show them the broken equipment, the dwindling supplies, the psychological wounds that never heal. Survival is not free—it is paid for in blood, in suffering, in pieces of the soul.

#### Corrupt or tempt

The Warp hungers for mortal souls, and Catachan is a place where the veil between reality and the immaterium is thin. Use this move to offer the characters power, knowledge, or survival at a terrible price. Show them the easy path, the shortcut that requires only a small compromise, the power that could save them all if they would just accept a little corruption. The Warp whispers promises of victory, of survival, of power beyond mortal comprehension—all it asks is their soul, their faith, their humanity. Make them choose between survival and damnation, between victory and corruption, between the Emperor's light and the darkness that promises to save them.

## Mission Moves

Mission Moves are a special subset that are used to make or alter a mission area on the fly. Use these if your players are exploring a hostile area that you don't already have planned completely.

Map out the area being explored as you make these moves. Most of them will require you to add a new location or element to your map.

- Change the environment
- Point to a looming threat
- Introduce a new faction or type of creature
- Use a threat from an existing faction or type of creature
- Make them backtrack
- Present riches at a price
- Present a challenge to one of the characters

You can make these moves whenever everyone looks to you to say something, when the players present you an opportunity, or when the players miss on a roll. They're particularly well-suited for when the characters enter a new area or location and want to know what they find there.

#### Change the environment

The environment is the general feel of the area the players are in: dense jungle, murky swamps, dark caves, or whatever else. This is your opportunity to introduce them to a new environment: the jungle gradually becomes swampy, the trees are dead and strange, or the trails are lost and the wilderness takes over. Use this move to vary the types of areas and creatures the players will face.

#### Point to a looming threat

If you know that something is lurking and waiting for the players to stumble upon it, this move shows them the signs and clues. This move is the Catachan Devil's tracks in the mud or the slimy trail of the death-spider.

#### Introduce a new faction or type of enemy

A type of enemy is a broad grouping: Orks, Tyranids, Chaos cultists, Catachan wildlife, etc.

A faction is a group of enemies united by a similar goal. Once you introduce them you can begin to make moves and cause trouble for the players with those enemies or NPCs.

Introducing means giving some clear sensory evidence or substantiated information. Don't be coy; the players should have some idea what you're showing the presence of. You can, however, be subtle in your approach. No need to have the cultist overlord waving a placard and screaming in the infernal tongue every single time.

A hard application of this move will snowball directly into a combat scene or ambush.

#### Use a threat from an existing faction or type of enemy

Once the characters have been introduced to the presence of a faction or type of enemy you can use moves of enemies of that type.

Use the factions and types broadly. Orks are accompanied with their grots and squigs. A mad cult probably has some daemon-possessed servants or maybe a few beasts summoned from the Warp. This is a move that, often, you'll be making subconsciously—it's just implementing the tools you've set out for yourself in a clear and effective manner.

#### Make them backtrack

Look back at the spaces you've added to the map. Is there anything useful there as yet undiscovered? Can you add a new obstacle that can only be overcome by going back there? Is there a locked door here and now whose key lies in an earlier location?

When backtracking, show the effect that time has had on the areas they've left behind. What new threats have sprung up in their wake? What didn't they take care of that's waiting for their return?

Use this move to make the mission area a living, breathing place. There is no stasis in the wake of the characters' passing. Add reinforcements, collapse structures, cause chaos. The jungle evolves in the wake of the characters' actions.

#### Present salvage at a price

What do the players want? What would they sacrifice for it?

Put some desirable item just out of reach. Find something they're short on: time, Wounds, gear, whatever. Find a way to make what they want available if they give up what they have.

The simplest way to use this move is the promise of salvage out of the way of the main objective. Will they stop to pry the valuable components from the wrecked vehicle when they know that the mission deadline looms closer and closer? Use this move and you can find out.

#### Present a challenge to one of the characters

Challenge a character by looking at what they're good at. Give the sniper a lock to pick, show the field medic wounded soldiers to treat. Give the sergeant tactical mysteries to investigate. Show the close combat specialist some skulls to crack. Give someone a chance to shine.

As an alternative, challenge a character by looking at what they're bad at or what they've left unresolved. If the scout has a complicated lie on his conscience, what steps will he take to cover it up when someone figures him out? If the heavy weapons specialist has been using unauthorized ammunition, what happens when command finds out?

This move can give a character the spotlight—even if just for a moment. Try to give everyone a chance to be the focus of play using this move from session to session.

## Dealing With Common Situations

There are some common situations that come up in Death World: Catachan. Here’s how to deal with them.

#### Fights

Sooner or later chainswords are drawn and blood is shed. When this happens the players are likely to start jungle fury, suppressive fire, and hold the line. Think about more than just the exchange of damage. Enemies might be trying to capture the characters or protect something from them. Understand what the fight is about; what each side wants and how that might affect the tide of battle.

No self-respecting enemy just stands still for their beating. Combat is a dynamic thing with creatures moving in and out of range, taking cover, and retreating. Sometimes the battlefield itself shifts. Have your enemies take action that the players will react to. Make sure you're making use of moves beyond deal damage, even in a fight.

Make sure everyone has a chance to act, and that you know where each player is during the chaos of combat. Make a map of a complex battle location so that everyone knows just what's happening and can describe their actions appropriately.

#### Traps

Traps may come from your prep, or you can improvise them based on your moves. If nothing has established that the location is safe, traps are always an option.

The players may find traps through clever plans, trap sense, or discerning realities. If a character describes an action that doesn’t trigger a move, but the action would still discover a trap, don’t hide it from them. Traps aren’t allowed to break the rules.

#### People

Tech-priests, medicae, soldiers of all shapes and sizes occupy the world around the characters. They're not mindless stooges to be pushed around but they're not what we're playing to find out about either. The NPCs are people: they have goals and the tools to struggle towards those goals. Use them to illustrate what the world is like. Show your players the common soldiers struggling for recognition or the officers seeking to maintain order. Some whole missions might take place in a peopled environment rather than an isolated jungle. Some specializations, the sergeant in particular, are adept at manipulating and using people as resources. Don't shy away from these situations. Be a fan of these characters, giving them interesting, nuanced people to interact with.

People, just like mission areas, change over time. The passing of the characters through their lives might inspire or enrage them. The characters' actions will cause the world to change, for good or ill, and the people they meet with will remember these changes. When the characters return to an outpost they were less-than-kind to on their previous visit, show them how the people are different now. Are they more cautious? Have they taken up new tactics? Are they hungry for revenge?

Relationships between characters are represented by Squad Cohesion but relationships with NPCs are more tenuous. If the players want to make real, lasting connections with the people of the world, they need to act. Remember, "what do you do?" is as valid a question when faced with the hopes and fears of a potential new ally or enemy as it is when staring down the business end of a chainsword.
