# First Session

The first session of a game of Death World: Catachan begins with character creation, the moment when souls are forged in the crucible of endless horror. Character creation is also world creation, the details on the character sheets and the questions that you ask establish what Death World: Catachan is like—who lives in it and what horrors await them, what darkness hungers for their souls.

This section is for the GM so it is addressed to you—the GM, the voice of the darkness, the herald of horror. For the players, the first session is just like every other, a moment in the perpetual conflict for survival. They just have to play their characters like real people and explore Death World: Catachan, to face the darkness with faith and fury. You have to do a little more in the first session. You establish the world and the threats the players will face, you manifest the unending terror of the Death World, you become the voice of the jungle that craves human flesh.

## Prep

Before the first session, you’ll need to print some stuff. Print off:

- A few copies of the basic moves
- One copy of each class sheet, double-sided
- One copy of each class move reference sheet, double-sided
- The GM sheet

You’ll also need to read this whole book, especially the sections on GMing (GM moves) and the basic moves. It’s a good idea to be acquainted with the class moves too, so you can be prepared for them. Be especially sure to read the rules for fronts, but don’t create any yet.

Think about grim worlds, alien horrors, and deadly threats. Think about the endless darkness that defines the 41st millennium, the horrors that lurk in every shadow, the abominations that hunger for human souls. Remember the games you played and the stories you told. Watch some movies, read some comics; get grim survival horror into your brain, let the darkness seep into your soul, for you must become the voice of the endless horror.

What you bring to the first session, ideas-wise, is up to you. At the very least bring your head full of ideas, of horrors that should not exist, of darkness that hungers for the light. That is the bare minimum.

If you like you can plan a little more. Maybe think of a threat and who is behind it, or some enemies you would like to use, some abominations that will challenge their devotion.

If you have got some spare time on your hands you can even draw some maps (but remember, from your principles: leave blanks) and imagine specific locations, places where the darkness runs deep, where horrors await the unwary.

The one thing you absolutely cannot bring to the table is a planned storyline or plot. You do not know the soldiers or the world before you sit down to play so planning anything concrete is just going to frustrate you. It also conflicts with your agenda: play to find out what happens, to discover how the characters will face the endless horror, whether they will survive or be consumed by the darkness.

### Getting Started

When everyone shows up for the first session briefly introduce Death World: Catachan to anyone who hasn’t played before. Cover the mechanical basis of moves. Introduce the character classes, help players pick their classes, and walk them through character creation.

Your role during character creation is threefold: help everyone, ask questions, and take notes. When a player makes a choice—particularly for establishing squad cohesion—ask them about it. Get more detail. Think about what these details mean.

You should also set expectations: the players are to play their characters as people—skilled soldiers on dangerous missions, but real people. Your role is to play the rest of the world as a dynamic, changing place.

Some questions commonly come up during character creation. You should be ready to answer them:

*Are the characters friends?*No, not necessarily, but they do work together as a team for common goals. Their reasons for pursuing those goals may be different, but they manage to work together.

*Are there other specialists like me?*Not really. There are other soldiers with similar training, but they're not like you. They don't have the same abilities, though they may be similar. Later on there may be another player character with the same class but no GM character will ever really be a Close Combat Specialist (or any other class).

*What's Respect?*Respect is how you get better equipment assigned to you. It's earned through service and reputation. It'll get you standard issue gear, like lasguns and flak armor. The special stuff, like relic weapons and ancient technology, isn't for assignment. Not for Respect, anyway…

*Is the GM trying to kill us?*No. The GM’s job is to portray the world and the things in it and the world is a very dangerous place. You might die. That doesn’t mean the GM is out to get you.

During this entire process, especially character creation, ask questions. Look for interesting facts established by the characters' shared history, moves, classes, and descriptions and ask about those things. Be curious! When someone mentions the Chaos that destroyed their outpost or the xenos that wiped out their squad, find out more about them. After all, you don't have anything (except maybe a mission area) and everything they give you is fuel for future missions.

Also pay attention to the players’ questions. When mechanical questions come up answer them. When questions of setting or fiction come up your best bet is to turn those questions around. When a player says, "Who is the Commissar of this sector," say, "I don't know. Who is it? What is he like?" Collaborate with your players. Asking a question means it’s something that interests them so work with them to make the answers interesting. Don’t be afraid to say, “I don’t know” and ask them the same questions. Work together to find a fantastic and interesting answer.

If you’ve come to the table with some ideas about stuff you’d like to see in the world, share them with the players. Their characters are their responsibility and the world is yours—you’ve got a lot of say in what lives in it. If you want the game to be about a hunt for lost technology or ancient xenos ruins, say so! If the players aren't interested or they're sick to death of that, they'll let you know and you can work together to find some other way. You don’t need pre-approval for everything but making sure everyone is excited about the broad strokes of the world is a great start.

Once everyone has their characters created you can take a deep breath. Look back over the questions you’ve asked and answered so far. You should have some notes that will point you towards what the game might look like. Look at what the players have brought to the table. Look to the ideas that’ve been stewing away in your head. It's time for the mission to begin!

### The First Adventure

The first mission is really about discovering the direction that future sessions will take. Throughout the first adventure keep your eye out for unresolved threats; note dangerous things that are mentioned but not dealt with. These will be fuel for sessions to come.

Start the session with a group of player characters (maybe all of them) in a tense situation, a moment where life and death hang in the balance. Use anything that demands action: outside the entrance to an abandoned outpost, the stench of death heavy on the air, ambushed in a fetid swamp, the water itself seeming to hunger for their flesh, peeking through the crack in a door at the Ork guards, their crude weapons dripping with blood, or being debriefed before the Commissar, his eyes burning with the fire of divine judgment. Ask questions right away—"who is leading the ambush against you?" or "what did you do to make the Commissar so mad?" If the situation stems directly from the characters and your questions, all the better, for it means the darkness has found them, and it will not let go.

Here’s where the game starts. The players will start saying and doing things, which means they’ll start making moves. For the first session you should watch especially carefully for when moves apply, until the players get the hang of it. Often, in the early sessions, the players will be most comfortable just narrating their actions—this is fine. When a move triggers let them know. Say, “It sounds like you’re trying to…” and then walk them through the move. Players looking for direction will look to their character sheet. When a player just says "I attack in melee" be quick to ask, "so what are you actually doing?" Ask "How?" or "With what?"

For the first session, you have a few specific goals:

- Establish details, describe
- Use what they give you
- Ask questions
- Leave blanks
- Look for interesting facts
- Help the players understand the moves
- Give each character a chance to shine
- Introduce NPCs

#### Establish details, describe

All the ideas and visions in your head don’t really exist in the fiction of the game until you share them, describe them, and detail them. The first session is the time to establish the basics of what things look like, who’s in charge, what they wear, what the world is like, what the immediate location is like. Describe everything but keep it brief enough to expand on later. Use a detail or two to make a description really stand out as real.

#### Use what they give you

The best part of the first session is you don’t have to come with anything concrete. You might have a mission area sketched out but the players provide the real meat—use it. They'll emerge from the darkness of that first mission and when they do and their eyes adjust to the light, you'll have built up an exciting world to explore with their help. Look at their squad cohesion, their moves, how they answer your questions and use what you find to fill in the world around the characters.

#### Ask questions

You’re using what they give you, right? What if you need more? That’s when you draw it out by asking questions. Poke and prod about specific things. Ask for reactions: “What does Lux think about that?” “Is Avon doing something about it?”

If you ever find yourself at a loss, pause for a second and ask a question. Ask one character a question about another. When a character does something, ask how a different character feels or reacts. Questions will power your game and make it feel real and exciting. Use the answers you find to fill in what might happen next.

#### Leave blanks

This is one of your principles, but it’s especially true during the first session. Every blank is another cool thing waiting to happen; leave yourself a stock of them.

#### Look for interesting facts

There are some ideas that, when you hear them, just jump out at you. When you hear one of those ideas, just write it down. When a player mentions the Chaos cultist being the one who corrupted their squad, note it. That little fact is the seed for a whole world.

#### Help the players understand the moves

You’ve already read the game, the players may not have, so it’s up to you to help them if they need it. The fact is, they likely won’t need it much. All they have to do is describe what their character does, the rules take care of the rest.

The one place they may need some help is remembering the triggers for the moves. Keep an ear out for actions that trigger moves, like attacking in melee or consulting their knowledge. After a few moves the players will likely remember them on their own.

#### Give each character a chance to shine

As a fan of the soldiers (remember your agenda?) you want to see them do what they do best. Give them a chance at this, not by tailoring every room to their skills, but by portraying a fantastic world (agenda again) where there are many solutions to every challenge.

#### Introduce NPCs

NPCs bring the world to life. If every enemy does nothing more than attack and every quartermaster sets out their wares for simple Respect the world is dead. Instead give your characters, especially those that the players show an interest in, life (principles, remember?). Introduce NPCs but don't protect them. The recently deceased Ork Warboss is just as useful for future missions as the one who's still alive.

## After the First Session

Once you’re done with the first session take some time to relax. Let ideas ferment. Don’t rush into the next session.

Once you’ve had some time to relax and think over the first session it’s time to prepare for the next session. Preparing for the second session takes a few minutes, maybe an hour if this is your first time. You’ll create fronts, maybe make some monsters or custom moves, and generally get an idea of what is going on in the world.
